Guide 01 · Basics

Speedcubing 101: what your child is actually doing

A speedcube is a mechanical puzzle. Scramble it — and the person who scrambles it makes sure it cannot be solved by accidentally reversing their moves — and then the task is to restore it using as few moves as possible, as fast as possible.

That last clause is where it stops being a puzzle and becomes a sport. Once your child can solve a cube in about a minute, they will immediately start trying to do it in thirty seconds. Then fifteen. Then eleven. Then they will want to be faster than their friends. You have, whether you intended it or not, created a measurable hobby.

What the WCA is

The World Cube Association governs competitive speedcubing. It sets the rules, runs the official event list, and maintains the world record list. It is a volunteer organisation and is entirely separate from any shop, brand or manufacturer — including us.

Official events include:

  • 3x3 — the main event, and by far the most popular.
  • 2x2 — a quarter of the pieces. Solves in seconds.
  • 4x4, 5x5, 6x6, 7x7 — bigger NxN cubes.
  • Pyraminx — four pieces. Frequently the first event anyone enters.
  • Megaminx — twelve faces. The 3x3 equivalent, times twelve.
  • Skewb, Square-1, Clock — the shape-shifting trio.

What “sub-30” means

You will hear numbers like “my PB is 14.2” or “I sub-20 my average”. This is a solve time in seconds. Sub-30 means averaging under thirty seconds. It is a small, self-contained language that lets children compare progress precisely — which, for some personalities, is the entire appeal.

The part parents usually care about

Cubing is a physical, screen-free, offline skill that requires sustained practice to improve. It is also one of the few hobbies where improvement is genuinely measurable by the person doing it. That combination is rare and it is why it sticks.

Why adults get competitive about it

Because it is one of the few hobbies where an hour of practice produces a visible, testable improvement, and almost nobody has to ask you to practise. It also has a genuinely international competitive circuit — a fifteen-year-old in Singapore and a fifteen-year-old in Brazil are solving the same puzzle under the same rules.

Guide 02 · Buying

The speedcube decoder

Every cube listing on this site — and every competitor listing anywhere — is built from the same four ingredients. Here is what each one does, and where it stops being worth the extra money.

Magnetic

Small magnets sit inside the pieces. Their job is simple: when a layer rotates far enough that it is roughly half-turn past the correct position, the magnet pulls it into alignment rather than letting it fly past. In practice this means a fast solver is less likely to blow up a solve.

Worth it for a beginner? Yes, marginally, but not because they will use the magnets. Because a magnetic cube that has been dropped is less likely to lose a piece, and a beginner will drop it.

MagLev

Instead of the metal springs that push each piece outward, a MagLev cube uses a ring of eight repelling neodymium magnets per corner. The pieces are held out by magnetic force rather than a spring.

The result is smoother, quieter and lighter, with no spring rattle and much less wear on the plastic. It is the single biggest quality jump in the hobby.

Worth it for a beginner? Not as the first cube. It is a reward, not a prerequisite — you cannot feel the difference until you can already solve.

UV coating

A liquid polymer applied over the plastic and cured under UV light. It adds gloss, improves finger grip, and resists scratching. It also adds roughly a gram of weight.

Worth it? Cosmetically yes, mechanically marginally. We think it is a reasonable thing to have and a poor reason to choose a cube.

Stickerless

The colours are moulded into the plastic itself rather than applied as stickers. This means the colours cannot peel or fade — stickers were routinely replaced every few months, which is why stickerless is now the standard on anything worth buying. It is slightly heavier than a stickered cube.

Guide 03 · Buying

Which cube should you buy first?

Three rules, in order of importance:

  1. Stickerless. If the cube has stickers on it, skip it. They peel and you will be re-stickering it within a month.
  2. Light and loose. A new cube should be on the looser side. It can be tightened later; it cannot be un-tightened if it is seized.
  3. Under $25. There is no reason to spend more on a first cube.

What to actually buy

A stickerless magnetic 3x3 in the $8–$15 range. Our default recommendation is the YuQi Hao RS3 M V5 — the tension is adjustable in seconds without taking it apart, which matters enormously when a nine-year-old has to do the adjusting themselves.

Three things not worth buying yet

  • A flagship GAN. Your child cannot yet feel the difference between a $12 and a $98 cube, and the flagship is harder to set up. Buy it as a reward, not a starting point.
  • A 4x4. A 4x4 requires the same method as a 3x3 plus an entirely separate set of moves. Buying one before they can comfortably solve a 3x3 buys frustration.
  • An oversize “training cube”. Sold as beginner-friendly. It is 10cm tall and cannot be timed properly, so it actively slows down the eventual transition to a real cube.

Worth adding now

  • A timing mat (~$15) — only once they are reliably under a minute. It transforms practice from guesswork into measurement.
  • A cheap 2x2 (~$8) — the pocket cube is easy to learn and genuinely fun. Good for a sibling who is not ready for a 3x3.
  • A second identical cube (~$12) — speedcubers practise by memorising, and having two makes side-by-side comparison possible.
Free setup, on us

Send the cube to us before a competition and we will tension it, lube it and spring-balance it at no charge. A cube set up properly will outlast one that has been assembled by hand in a bedroom about three to one.

Guide 04 · Care

Lubricant & maintenance

Is speedcube lubricant safe?

Yes, with a caveat worth stating plainly. Cubing lubricants are pure synthetic hydrocarbon fluids — typically Krytox or similar. They are chemically inert, non-toxic, odourless, and will not damage ABS plastic.

The caveat: they are not the same product as the “food-grade, non-toxic, safe for babies” silicone sold in a pharmacy. If a child is going to put anything near their mouth, use a product explicitly labelled for it, and keep the applicator out of reach. Otherwise, normal use — including handling by small hands — is not a concern.

Is it necessary?

Not on day one. A new cube arrives factory-lubed and is perfectly playable. Lubricant becomes necessary when you notice the first symptoms:

  • The cube feels gritty or scratchy rather than smooth.
  • Pieces have started swelling or discolouring from friction.
  • Turns feel heavy and sticky at the corners.

At that point lubrication will revive it. Skipping it for a year can end with a permanently swollen, unfixable cube. This is the single most common way an otherwise good cube gets thrown away.

The two-minute routine

  1. Disassemble the cube. It comes apart with the turn of a finger if it is a stickerless cube — no tools.
  2. Wash the pieces in warm water with a drop of dish soap. This removes years of grime from the inside of the mechanism.
  3. Dry completely. Water trapped inside a cube is the fastest way to destroy it.
  4. Reassemble, turning each layer roughly ten times to seat the pieces.
  5. Two drops of lube per corner. That is genuinely all of it. More lube is worse than no lube.
Cleaning is the part people skip

The single highest-return intervention on an old cube is a thorough wash with soap and water. Grit removed mechanically does more for the feel of a cube than any amount of lubricant, and it costs nothing.

Guide 05 · Community

Attending a first WCA competition

How Singapore competitions run

Singapore runs a full calendar of WCA-sanctioned competitions, typically at community clubs, schools and polytechnics. Registration is through the WCA competition page for the specific event. Events are usually free or cost a small entry fee.

What a competition day looks like

An average event has somewhere between twenty and a hundred competitors. The day divides roughly into:

  • Check-in — arrive, register, collect the scramble sheets and wristbands.
  • Rounds — several rounds per event. An Ao5 (average of five) is the standard format: solve five, drop your fastest and slowest, average the remaining three.
  • Results — published on the WCA results page and the live results screen, usually within the hour.

There is a strict no-phone rule in the solving area. Competitors keep phones in bags. This is the closest thing the hobby has to a dress code and it is enforced strictly.

Registration, mechanically

  1. Find the event on the WCA competition calendar.
  2. Click Register on the event page and complete the WCA account details.
  3. Choose your events. Note that you can enter as many events as you like.
  4. Confirm, and turn up at least thirty minutes early for your first one.

How to be the parent every cuber wishes they had

  • Learn one term. Just the event name and “PB”. It is a free, disproportionate goodwill signal.
  • Do not coach in the solving area. Competing is the one time a cuber cannot have help, and most find it genuinely difficult.
  • Ask before touching the cube. Every cube has been tuned specifically for that competition, and well-meaning “let me just loosen that” can ruin a morning.
  • Bring food and patience. A competition is a long, slow, social day. Treat it as a day out, not an exam.
The real point

Almost nobody's child finishes first. The reason cubers keep coming back is that the community is one of the friendliest in competitive sport, and that feeling is created almost entirely by the adults who turn up.
